# Paul Matthis is the PI's brother — what that does and does not require

Working note. Not a submission document. Nothing in the proposal changes.

## Short version

Hiring a qualified sibling is legal and ordinary in a small nonprofit. It is
not a problem to be hidden — it is a governance step to be documented. There
are two separate obligations, and they live in different places.

## 1. NSF-facing: one line in the COA

Paul goes in **your COA Table 2** — "names for whom a personal, family, or
business relationship would otherwise preclude their service as a reviewer" —
with the Type of Relationship column reading **family (brother)**.

That is the entire NSF-facing disclosure. Notably:

- COA is a **Single Copy Document**. Reviewers never see it. This does not go
  in front of the panel.
- Nothing in the PAPPG or NSF 26-506 requires disclosing a family relationship
  among project personnel anywhere in the proposal narrative, the budget
  justification, or the Other Personnel biographical document.
- Paul is Other Personnel (Line B), not senior/key, so he does not file his own
  COA.

**Do not** add a family disclosure to the budget justification or the Other
Personnel document. It is not required there, and volunteering it in a
reviewer-visible document invites a question the reviewers have no role in
answering.

## 2. Organization-facing: this is the part that actually matters

### Why

Under IRC §4958, a **family member of a disqualified person is themselves a
disqualified person**. You are President of the Foundation, which makes you a
disqualified person. Your brother therefore is one too. Paying him $44,250 of
award funds is a transaction with a disqualified person, and those must be
demonstrably reasonable or they become excess-benefit transactions — which
carry personal excise tax liability for the individuals involved, not just
organizational risk.

2 CFR 200.318(c)(1) points the same direction for immediate-family conflicts.
It is written for procurement rather than salaried employees, so it is not
squarely on point for a Line B employee, but auditors reason from it.

### What protects you

The good news: **the budget already contains most of the defense.** The
"rebuttable presumption of reasonableness" procedure wants three things:

1. **Comparability data.** ✅ Already done — Paul's rate is benchmarked against
   the 75th-percentile BLS OEWS figure for SOC 15-1244, with a live citation,
   exactly as the PESOSE guidance requires.
2. **Approval by disinterested board members**, with the interested party
   recused. ❓ Needs to happen and be minuted. You must recuse.
3. **Contemporaneous documentation** of the basis for the decision. ❓ The
   written compensation policy is still `written-compensation-policy-DRAFT.md`.

Items 2 and 3 are the gaps. Neither blocks submission — they matter before the
Foundation expends award funds, and they matter a great deal if anyone ever
looks.

### The AOR certification

At submission the AOR completes a **Certification Regarding Conflict of
Interest**, stating that the organization has implemented and is enforcing a
written COI policy and that conflicts will be managed before funds are spent.

PAPPG Chapter IX.A.1 imposes the written-policy mandate on organizations
"employing more than fifty persons." The Foundation employs three, so that
mandate does not bind you. But the AOR still signs the certification, and if
you are also the AOR you are certifying about your own brother's salary.

**Ask your AOR/SPO how they want this handled.** That is not a formality — it
is the one place where the relationship touches something you personally sign.

## What to do, in order

1. Add Paul to COA Table 2 as **family (brother)**. Takes ten seconds.
2. Before spending award funds: board approves Paul's compensation with you
   recused, minuted, citing the BLS benchmark already in the budget.
3. Adopt the compensation policy so it stops being a draft.
4. Raise the AOR COI certification with whoever signs it.

Only step 1 is needed before submission.
