# Summary (5000 Characters)

The FreeMoCap X-Lab will develop and disseminate a new class of scientific instrument — the Densely Overlapping Measurement Environment (DOME) — that records the complete perception–action loop of a behaving agent as a single calibrated, uncertainty-tagged measurement. Every agent, living or engineered, solves one problem: sense the world through imperfect transducers and push its body against it to reach a goal. We can already measure each thread of that loop with great precision — body kinematics, gaze, neural activity, muscle force, contact forces, and the surrounding scene — but these instruments do not compose. Each carries its own coordinate frame, clock, and vocabulary, and none propagates uncertainty across a modality boundary, so the coherent whole stays out of reach. The DOME closes this gap: defined by the measurement rather than by any single sensor, it yields the same canonical, semantically-unified record no matter which instruments are present, and becomes a boundary object aligning perceptual and motor neuroscience, musculoskeletal biomechanics, mobile robotics, and embodied AI into one convergent science of natural behavior.

The build is organized around a single demanding benchmark: reconstructing the visual input to a moving agent's retina precisely enough to predict the neural activity it drives — gaze-in-world to better than 1° (roughly the diameter of the human fovea), and eye kinematics fine enough to recover retinal optic flow and its curl. Meeting it means composing three measurement threads to specified precision. For the body, a self-calibrating, actuated camera array retunes its own optics to cover any capture volume and fuses with inertial capture into one uncertainty-tagged estimate, dissolving the calibration bottleneck that freezes large-volume rigs. For the eye, a next-generation dense-sensor-array tracker measures ocular torsion — a degree of freedom no existing tracker captures, yet one that shapes the retinal-flow signal implicated in real-world locomotion. For the world, a head-mounted scanner reconstructs the scene in the agent's own coordinate frame, extended outdoors by drone-swarm nodes and, in the flagship, an actuated floor that turns the ground into a controllable stimulus. These are genuinely new sensing instruments — new transducers, not only new software.

The same calibrated record unblocks fields stalled for want of it. Computer-vision pose estimation needs physical ground truth at scale, and legged-robot reinforcement learning needs uncertainty-tagged recordings of real agents to close the sim-to-real gap; the DOME produces both by construction. Because the record is defined by a shared sensor-grounded ontology, a reconstructed body or retinal input denotes the same quantity whether it comes from a human, a ferret, a marmoset, or a guinea fowl — making the DOME one instrument spanning species, scale, and setting. It also generates its own training signal, back-projecting calibrated 3D reconstructions to fine-tune pose estimators on the out-of-distribution movement that matters most; in prior work this drove per-limb tracking error from 152 mm to under 15 mm. Over five to seven years we deliver a warehouse-scale flagship (DOME-L), a disseminated lab- and classroom-scale form (DOME-S), and a wearable field form (DOME-Mobile), each cross-validated against the flagship, shipped with build guides any lab can follow, with clinical rigor anchored to FDA 510(k) clearance.

This instrument is unmet by existing structures because building durable instruments is a distinct craft that academic incentives cannot sustain — the measurement needs its own organization. The FreeMoCap Foundation, an autonomous 501(c)(3), already ran this experiment, bringing open, world-class motion capture to over 15,000 users across 152 countries. The X-Lab applies that same measurement-scoped, master-built, full-time-maintained model to the far larger target of the whole interaction loop. Its leadership pairs perception–action neuroscience and clinical biomechanics with enterprise software architecture, applied-AI systems, and mechatronics engineering, run by dedicated operations and finance leaders, and already functions as a single team around a shipping instrument. A standing network of collaborators across neuroscience, biomechanics, and robotics executes the domain science, and a global community sets priorities through an open request-for-comments process and twice-yearly gatherings. Because the sensing rides commodity-imager cost curves and the grant-funded organization depends on no hardware margin, the DOME can reach every classroom, clinic, and field site that needs it — the collapse by which a measurement becomes a platform, and the study of natural behavior becomes one accumulating, connected science.
